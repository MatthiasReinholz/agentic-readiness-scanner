## In short

Before a shopper commits, they want to know whether they can send the product back. Agents that shop on someone's behalf need the same answer, and merchant programmes ask for a return policy URL. This check follows the return or refund link on your home page and passes when it leads to a readable policy page with more than 100 words.

## What this check looks at

The scanner reads the home page as the server delivers it and ranks every link that could be the return policy. A link qualifies when its label or the last part of its path names the topic as a whole word. The vocabulary covers several languages:

| Language | Words matched                                                |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| English  | returns, refunds, exchanges, withdrawal                      |
| German   | Widerruf, Widerrufsbelehrung, Rückgabe, Rücksendung, Retoure |
| French   | retour                                                       |
| Italian  | reso, resi, rimborso                                         |
| Spanish  | devolución, reembolso                                        |

When several links match, the scanner prefers a label made only of policy words ("Returns & refunds") over a label that also carries other words, a footer link over a link in the page body, and a label match over a path match. Product, category and account URLs are never chosen, whatever their label says.

On Shopify, the platform serves its own refund policy at `/policies/refund-policy`. When no link was found, or the best link matched only weakly (for example by its path alone), the scanner uses that page if it exists.

When the home page links no return policy at all, the scanner looks in your sitemap. A URL on your own site whose last path segment names the policy document, such as `/returns-policy` or `/refund-policy`, is used instead. The page is still fetched and judged like a linked one.

## Why it matters for AI agents

An agent comparing two shops for a shopper will weigh return terms the way a person does. It cannot weigh what it cannot find. The home page is the natural starting point, and the footer is where people and machines look for policies.

Every checkout programme the scanner tracks asks for a return policy URL. That includes Google Merchant Center and UCP (Universal Commerce Protocol, an open standard for agents to discover and buy from stores), the OpenAI product feed, Stripe, and Shopify's sales channels. Google Merchant Center's checkout requirements say customers must be able to find refund and return policy information.

Structured data (machine-readable facts embedded in the page) can describe return terms too, through schema.org `MerchantReturnPolicy`. The [shipping and return markup check](/knowledge-base/shipping-and-return-markup) covers that. The page itself remains the human-readable source that the markup links to.

This check carries weight 3, which means blocking. It is part of the Level 3 gate, "Trusted merchant". A store cannot reach Level 3 until this check passes. Every agent checkout programme asks for a return policy before it lists a store, so trust comes before product identity in the levels.

## How the scanner decides

The scanner fetches the linked page once.

- **Pass:** the page answers HTTP 200, is not a product page, and has more than 100 words of text.
- **Warn:** the page answers but has 100 words or fewer. A stub like "Contact us for returns" is not a policy.
- **Fail:** no qualifying link was found on the home page, the page did not answer HTTP 200, or the only matching link leads to a product page. A product page is recognised by Product JSON-LD (structured data that describes a product) or an `og:type` of product.
- **Skipped as inconclusive:** the page refused the scanner (for example 403 or a bot challenge), was rate limited, or failed temporarily (no answer, or 502, 503 or 504). A refusal says nothing about the policy, so it is never a fail and drops out of the score. The scanner still does not award Level 3 on a page it could not read. When nothing else stands in the way, the report says the level could not be decided rather than that it was missed.

The word count covers the visible text of the whole page, outside scripts, styles and similar elements. The check does not judge what the policy says, only that a real page exists and has substance.

Links are read from the server-rendered HTML. A footer that is built in the browser by JavaScript is invisible to this check, as it is to many crawlers. When the whole home page is an empty shell that JavaScript fills in (fewer than 3 links and fewer than 50 words in the HTML), the failure message says so. Your policy may well exist for people. An agent that reads HTML without running scripts still has no link to follow, so for that agent the policy does not exist.

## How to fix it

A return policy only counts for agents when a machine can find it and read it. That takes five things:

1. **A link in the HTML.** Link the page from the footer of every page, in the HTML the server sends, not only in a menu that JavaScript builds.
2. **Its own URL.** Give the policy a stable URL that answers HTTP 200, while URLs that do not exist answer 404.
3. **The text in the HTML.** The policy text must be in the page's HTML. A page that loads its text with JavaScript is empty for most AI fetchers.
4. **Open to crawlers.** Do not disallow the policy path in robots.txt, and do not mark it `noindex`. The [agent-readable policy pages check](/knowledge-base/agent-readable-policy-pages) tests both.
5. **In your sitemap and your markup.** List the URL in your sitemap. Point `merchantReturnLink` in your `MerchantReturnPolicy` markup at it, so search engines and agents get the terms as data too.

Write a return policy that answers the questions a shopper asks: how many days they have, what condition the item must be in, who pays return shipping, how and when the refund is paid, and how to start a return. Legal requirements for returns and withdrawal depend on the jurisdiction, so check what applies where you sell. Then link the page from the footer of every page with a clear label such as "Returns" or "Refunds".

### WordPress and WooCommerce

- WooCommerce creates a draft "Refund and Returns Policy" page on installation in recent versions. Look for it under Pages, complete it and publish it.
- Add it to the footer menu under Appearance, Menus, or in the footer template part in the Site Editor for block themes.
- Keep the label short and plain. "Returns" works better than "Peace of mind guarantee".

### Shopify

- Write the policy under Settings, Policies, Refund policy. Shopify publishes it at `/policies/refund-policy`.
- Link it in the footer menu under Online Store, Navigation. The scanner can find the Shopify page without a link, but people and other agents rely on the footer.
- Shopify marks its policy pages as noindex by default. That does not fail this check, but the [agent-readable policy pages check](/knowledge-base/agent-readable-policy-pages) warns about it.

### Any other stack

- Publish the policy at a stable, descriptive URL such as `/returns` or `/refund-policy`.
- Link it from a `footer` element in the server-rendered HTML of the home page.
- Reference the same URL from `merchantReturnLink` in your `MerchantReturnPolicy` markup so the page and the structured data agree.

## Verify it yourself

Look for return links in the home page as the server sends it:

```bash
curl -s https://example.com/ | grep -oiE 'href="[^"]*"[^>]*>[^<]*(return|refund|widerruf|retour)[^<]*' | head
```

Then fetch the page you found and count its words:

```bash
curl -s https://example.com/returns/ | sed -e 's/<[^>]*>/ /g' | wc -w
```

The count includes navigation text, so it is an upper bound. Rescan with this scanner after the change.

## Common mistakes

- **Returns information only in an FAQ accordion.** A single answer inside a long FAQ page can be found, but a dedicated page is clearer for people and machines.
- **A footer built by JavaScript.** If the links only appear after scripts run, the server-rendered home page has none.
- **A product named after the topic.** A link to a "Return label printer" product page is not a policy. The scanner fails it on purpose.
- **A placeholder page.** A page that says only "Coming soon" or two sentences warns.
- **Linking the policy from checkout only.** Agents and shoppers look before they reach checkout.

## FAQ

### Where should the return policy link go?

In the footer of every page, with a plain label such as "Returns" or "Refund policy". That is where people expect it and where the scanner gives a link the most weight.

### Does a return policy have to be a separate page?

For this check, yes. The link must lead to a page that is not a product page and has more than 100 words. A combined "Shipping and returns" page works for both this and the shipping check.

### My store has a return policy. Why does the scanner say it found none?

Because an agent reading your home page could not find a link to it either. The usual causes are a footer built by JavaScript, a page whose links all appear only after scripts run, or a policy reachable only from a help centre on another path. The message says when your home page is an empty shell. Link the policy in the server-rendered footer, or at least list it in your sitemap at a URL that names the document, such as `/returns-policy`, and rescan.

### Does the scanner check whether my policy is legally compliant?

No. It checks that a policy page exists, is linked and has substance. What a policy must contain depends on the jurisdiction and is a question for a legal adviser.

### Do I still need the page if I have MerchantReturnPolicy markup?

Yes. The markup summarises your terms for machines. The page is the full policy that people read and that merchant programmes ask you to link.
