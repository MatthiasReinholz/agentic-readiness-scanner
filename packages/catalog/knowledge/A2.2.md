## In short

AI assistants answer shopping questions from search indexes that crawlers, programs that visit pages on their own schedule, build in advance. If robots.txt disallows those crawlers on your product pages, the assistants built on them cannot cite or recommend those pages. This check reads your robots.txt the way each of eleven search and assistant crawlers would and reports which of them may read a sampled product page.

## What this check looks at

robots.txt is the plain-text file at the root of a host, for example `https://example.com/robots.txt`, that tells crawlers what they may fetch. It is made of groups. Each group names one or more crawlers in `User-agent` lines, by their product token such as `Googlebot`, and then lists `Allow` and `Disallow` path rules. RFC 9309, the Robots Exclusion Protocol, defines how a crawler reads it.

Two rules from that standard explain most surprises:

- **A named group replaces the wildcard group.** A crawler that finds a group with its own name follows only that group and ignores `User-agent: *` entirely. A group written for one bot can therefore block or open far more than intended.
- **The longest matching path wins.** `Allow: /products/` beats `Disallow: /` for a product URL. When an `Allow` and a `Disallow` rule are equally long, `Allow` wins.

The check evaluates these crawlers, grouped by the answers they feed:

| Crawler          | What it feeds                                                                        |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Googlebot        | Google Search, AI Overviews and AI Mode                                              |
| Bingbot          | Bing, Microsoft Copilot, and ChatGPT search results that come from Microsoft's index |
| Storebot-Google  | Google's shopping surfaces                                                           |
| Applebot         | Siri, Spotlight and Apple Intelligence                                               |
| OAI-SearchBot    | ChatGPT search                                                                       |
| Claude-SearchBot | Claude's search results                                                              |
| PerplexityBot    | Perplexity answers                                                                   |
| Amzn-SearchBot   | Alexa and Amazon search                                                              |
| DuckAssistBot    | DuckDuckGo's AI-assisted answers                                                     |
| MistralAI-Index  | Mistral search                                                                       |
| meta-webindexer  | Meta AI search citations                                                             |

## Why it matters for AI agents

An assistant that answers "which store sells this jacket in my size" does not crawl the web at that moment. It looks the question up in an index that a crawler filled earlier. A product page that the crawler was told not to read is missing from that index, or is reduced to a bare link. Perplexity, for example, documents that a page its crawler may not read can only appear with its domain, headline and a short summary.

Googlebot and Bingbot matter most. Google's AI features answer from the Google index, and OpenAI documents that ChatGPT search draws on Microsoft's index among its providers. Blocking either one removes a store from several assistants at once.

These crawlers are not training crawlers. GPTBot, ClaudeBot, Google-Extended and similar tokens control whether content may train models, and that is a separate decision the [training-crawler policy check](/knowledge-base/ai-training-crawler-policy) looks at. You can block training and still be found in AI answers.

## How the scanner decides

The scanner fetches robots.txt once, picks the path of a sampled product page (the home page when it found no product), and asks for each crawler whether that path is allowed under RFC 9309.

- **Pass:** every listed crawler may read the path, or the site has no robots.txt rules at all.
- **Warn:** exactly one of the crawlers other than Googlebot and Bingbot is disallowed.
- **Fail:** Googlebot or Bingbot is disallowed, or two or more of the other crawlers are.
- **Skip:** robots.txt answered with a server error or an HTML page, so the [robots.txt check](/knowledge-base/robots-txt-exists-and-parses) failed and there are no rules to read.

The evidence in the report lists every crawler with its verdict and what it feeds, so you can see at a glance which assistants are affected.

This check is a Level 1 gate, and a level needs every gate to pass. A warning or a fail here holds the store at Level 0 and caps the scores (ingestion at 40, commerce at 30), because nothing else on the page matters when the crawlers behind AI answers may not read it.

## How to fix it

Decide first which paths should stay private. Cart, checkout, account and internal search pages are usually disallowed for everyone, and that is fine. Product, category and policy pages should be open.

### WordPress and WooCommerce

- WordPress serves a virtual robots.txt unless a physical `robots.txt` file exists in the web root. Check for that file first, because it overrides everything else.
- SEO plugins such as Yoast SEO and Rank Math include a robots.txt editor. Make the change there so it survives theme and plugin updates.
- Security and "block bad bots" plugins sometimes add disallow groups for AI crawlers. Review their settings after you edit the file.

### Shopify

- Shopify generates robots.txt from the theme template `robots.txt.liquid`. Without that template you get Shopify's defaults, which allow these crawlers.
- If the template exists, open it under Online Store, Themes, Edit code, and look for groups that name any of the crawlers above.
- Apps that promise to "block AI bots" often edit this template. Remove the groups for search and assistant crawlers and keep only those for training crawlers, if that is your intent.

### Any other stack

- Edit robots.txt where your platform or web server generates it.
- If a CDN manages robots.txt for you, check its settings as well. Cloudflare, for example, offers a managed robots.txt that can add AI-crawler rules on top of yours.
- Keep one group per intent: one for training crawlers, one for search and assistant crawlers, and the wildcard group for everyone else.

## Verify it yourself

Read the live file and look for groups that name the crawlers:

```bash
curl -s https://example.com/robots.txt
```

Then check a product URL against a specific crawler with Google's robots.txt report in Search Console, or Bing Webmaster Tools' robots.txt tester. Rescan with this scanner after a few minutes to confirm the verdict changed.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing a group for one AI bot and forgetting the paths.** `User-agent: PerplexityBot` followed only by `Disallow: /cart/` is fine. Followed by `Disallow: /`, it removes the store from Perplexity answers.
- **Treating every AI user agent the same.** Blocking "all AI bots" with one list usually catches the search crawlers along with the training crawlers.
- **Fixing robots.txt on one host only.** `www.example.com` and `example.com` each serve their own robots.txt. Product pages are judged by the file on the host that serves them.
- **Relying on robots.txt alone.** A crawler that may read a page can still be stopped by a firewall challenge. The [honest fetch check](/knowledge-base/no-block-on-honest-fetch) covers that.

## FAQ

### Does allowing OAI-SearchBot let OpenAI train on my content?

No. OpenAI documents OAI-SearchBot for search and GPTBot for training as separate crawlers with separate robots.txt controls. Allowing one says nothing about the other.

### I block GPTBot and ClaudeBot. Does this check fail?

No. Training crawlers are not part of this check. Blocking them never costs points anywhere in the scanner.

### Why do Googlebot and Bingbot count more than the others?

Their indexes feed the most AI answers. Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode use Google's index, and Copilot and ChatGPT search rely on Microsoft's. Losing either one removes the store from several assistants at once.

### My robots.txt has no rules for these crawlers. Is that a problem?

No. A crawler without a group of its own follows the wildcard group. If `User-agent: *` allows product pages, every crawler on the list may read them.
