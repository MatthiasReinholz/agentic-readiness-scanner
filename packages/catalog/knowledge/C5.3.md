## In short

If your store shows customer reviews, EU consumer law treats one piece of information as essential: whether and how you check that the reviews come from people who actually bought or used the product. This check looks for that statement on the pages it reads when reviews are present. It cannot tell whether your checking process exists or works.

## What this check looks at

The check concerns three markets, each with its own rule about reviews.

- **European Union.** Article 7(6) of the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (UCPD), added by the Omnibus Directive (EU) 2019/2161, makes this information material: when a trader gives access to consumer reviews, whether and how it ensures the reviews come from consumers who used or bought the product. Annex I of the UCPD lists practices that are unfair in all circumstances. Point 23b covers stating that reviews come from real users without taking reasonable and proportionate steps to check. Point 23c covers submitting or commissioning fake reviews and misrepresenting reviews. The rules have applied since 28 May 2022 through national law, in Germany for example in the Unfair Competition Act (UWG).
- **United Kingdom.** Schedule 20, paragraph 13 of the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 (DMCC Act) has applied since 6 April 2025. It bans fake reviews, concealed incentivised reviews and publishing reviews or review information in a misleading way, and it expects publishers to take proportionate steps to prevent and remove such reviews. The Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) explains those steps in its fake reviews guidance, CMA208.
- **United States.** The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) Trade Regulation Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials, 16 CFR Part 465, has applied since 21 October 2024. It prohibits fake reviews, buying reviews that must be positive or negative, undisclosed reviews by insiders and suppressing negative reviews. It does not ask for a verification statement, so in the US this check is about consistency rather than a disclosure duty.

The report lists each of these provisions with a link to its official text.

## Why it matters for AI agents

Answer engines and shopping agents summarise reviews. They read the star rating and the review count from `AggregateRating` markup, and they turn review text into lines such as "customers say it runs small". They repeat what they find. If the reviews are unchecked or curated, the summary carries that bias into every answer, with your store's name on it.

An agent cannot inspect your moderation process. The verification statement is the only evidence about review quality that a machine can read, and it is the context a person needs when an agent quotes your rating. A clear "reviews come from verified orders" line travels with the summary. Silence leaves the rating unexplained.

Consistency matters as much. When the markup claims 412 reviews and the page shows 37, an agent cannot tell which number is true. Search engines expect markup to reflect what the page shows, as the [markup matches the visible page check](/knowledge-base/markup-matches-visible-page) explains, and a mismatch makes every rating on the site look less reliable.

## How the scanner decides

The check runs only when the home page or a sampled product page carries reviews: `AggregateRating` or `Review` markup, or a known review widget such as Trustpilot, Judge.me, Yotpo, Reviews.io, Okendo, Stamped, Loox, Feefo, Trusted Shops, eKomi, Bazaarvoice or PowerReviews.

- **Found:** the home page, a sampled product page or the terms page explains how reviews are checked. The scanner looks for wording such as "verified purchase", "verified buyer", "how we check reviews", "Bewertungen werden geprüft" or "avis vérifiés".
- **Review:** no such statement is found on those pages.
- **Not applicable:** no reviews were detected on the pages read.

What the scanner cannot see:

- Review widgets usually load their content with JavaScript. A "Verified buyer" badge drawn inside the widget is invisible to a scan that reads the HTML the server sends.
- Any other page, such as a separate review policy page. Put at least one sentence of the statement next to the reviews on the product page.
- Whether the `AggregateRating` counts match the reviews the page shows. The scanner does not compare them.
- Whether the process the statement describes is real, complete or applied to every review.

The result is reported, never scored, and it is not legal advice.

## How to fix it

Write down what you actually do, then put a short version next to the reviews and the full version on its own page. Cover whether you check reviews, how you match them to orders, how you treat incentivised reviews, whether you publish negative reviews and when you remove a review. If you do not check reviews, the statement should say that too.

### WordPress and WooCommerce

- Under WooCommerce, Settings, Products, WooCommerce offers a "verified owner" label on reviews and an option to accept reviews only from verified owners. Turn them on if they match your process, and describe them in the statement.
- Create a page such as "How we check reviews" and link it from the reviews tab, for example with a short paragraph added through your theme's single-product template or a block in the product template of a block theme.
- If a review plugin replaces the WooCommerce reviews, check whether it has its own disclosure text field. Make sure the sentence also appears in the server-rendered HTML, not only inside the plugin's script.

### Shopify

- Create the full statement under Online Store, Pages.
- In the theme editor, open the product template and add a text or custom Liquid block next to the reviews app block with one sentence and a link to that page.
- Review apps render their widgets with JavaScript. Their own "verified" badges help people but do not reach a scan of the HTML, so the static sentence is what makes the statement visible to machines.

### Any other stack

- Render the statement and the link server-side in the product template, directly before or after the review list.
- Generate `reviewCount` and `ratingCount` from the same query that renders the reviews, so the markup and the page cannot drift apart.
- Do not mark up a shop-wide rating from a third-party platform as the rating of an individual product.

## Verify it yourself

Look for verification wording and for the counts in the markup of a product page:

```bash
curl -s https://example.com/products/example | grep -ioE "verified (purchase|buyer|customer)|how we (check|verify) reviews|bewertungen werden geprüft|avis vérifiés"
curl -s https://example.com/products/example | grep -oE "\"(reviewCount|ratingCount|ratingValue)\"[^,}]*"
```

No output from the first command means the statement is not in the HTML the server sends. Compare the counts from the second command with the number of reviews the page shows, then rescan.

## Common mistakes

- **Statement only inside the widget.** A badge rendered by a review app's script does not reach crawlers, agents or this scan.
- **Claiming more checking than happens.** "All reviews are verified" while imported or unmatched reviews are shown is exactly the kind of statement the EU blacklist addresses.
- **Statement buried in the terms.** A clause in the terms of service is hard for people to find next to the reviews. Put a sentence where the reviews are.
- **Stale counts in markup.** A cached `reviewCount` that no longer matches the page.
- **Hiding negative reviews.** Publishing only positive reviews distorts the rating whatever the statement says, and the rules in all three markets address suppressing or misrepresenting reviews.

## FAQ

### Does this apply to my store?

The EU provision concerns traders that give consumers access to reviews of their products, and it has no size threshold. The UK rules apply to traders and publishers of consumer reviews dealing with consumers in the UK. The US rule applies to businesses generally. Whether your store sells to consumers in these markets, and how the rules apply to it, is a question for a qualified lawyer.

### Do I have to verify every review?

EU law asks you to say whether and how you check reviews. It does not prescribe one method. Claiming that reviews come from real customers without reasonable and proportionate steps to check is on the EU blacklist, so the statement must match the process.

### My review app shows a "Verified buyer" badge. Why does the scan say Review?

The badge is drawn by the app's JavaScript after the page loads. The scanner reads the HTML the server sends and does not run scripts. Add a short server-rendered sentence and a link to your review policy next to the reviews.

### Does the scanner compare my review counts with the page?

No. It does not compare `AggregateRating` counts with the reviews shown. Align them anyway: when the markup says one number and the page shows another, people and agents cannot tell which is true.
