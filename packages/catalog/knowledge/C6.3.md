## In short

Many AI image tools mark their output as generated, with a signed C2PA manifest (Content Credentials) or the IPTC metadata value `trainedAlgorithmicMedia`. Resizing, optimisation and CDNs often strip that metadata before the image reaches a shopper. This check reads the main image of up to two product pages and reports when an image still declares AI generation. Missing metadata proves nothing, so the check otherwise does not apply.

## What this check looks at

This check concerns the EU.

- **Marking.** Article 50(2) of the AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, requires providers of AI systems that generate synthetic images, audio, video or text to mark the output in a machine-readable format so that it can be detected as artificially generated. It has applied since 2 August 2026. Under the Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, systems placed on the market before that date have until 2 December 2026. It is a provider duty: it binds the maker of the image generator, not the store that uses it.
- **Deep fakes.** Article 50(4) requires deployers of an AI system that generates a deep fake to disclose that the content is artificially generated or manipulated. The AI Act defines a deep fake as generated or manipulated content that resembles existing persons, objects, places, entities or events and would falsely appear to a person to be authentic. Whether a given product image falls under that definition depends on the image and how it is used.

The two metadata formats the scanner reads:

- **C2PA manifest.** The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) specification embeds a cryptographically signed record of how an image was made and edited. Many generators, Adobe Firefly and OpenAI's image models among them, attach one.
- **IPTC DigitalSourceType.** The IPTC (International Press Telecommunications Council) digital source type vocabulary describes where an image came from. `trainedAlgorithmicMedia` means created by a generative model. `compositeWithTrainedAlgorithmicMedia` means a composite that includes generated elements. The value is stored in the image's embedded XMP metadata.

The report lists the provision with a link to its official text.

## Why it matters for AI agents

Shopping agents and answer engines show product images to people and use them to describe products. Provenance metadata lets them tell a photograph of the actual product from a generated illustration. Platforms already read it: some show labels such as "AI info" or "Made with AI" based on C2PA or IPTC data. An agent that sees the marker can present the image as an illustration. An agent that sees nothing has to guess, and the safest guess for it is to treat every image as a photograph.

For a store, keeping the marker is low effort and keeps the record straight. A shopper who later learns that the photo of a jacket was generated, while an agent presented it as real, blames the store rather than the agent. The marker travels with the file to every marketplace, feed and agent that copies it. Image quality matters for agents as well, as the [product image alt text and resolution check](/knowledge-base/product-image-alt-text-and-resolution) explains.

## How the scanner decides

The check runs when the store appears to sell to the EU. The scanner takes the main image of up to two sampled product pages and fetches it, up to 2 MB each. It looks in the file for the IPTC values `trainedAlgorithmicMedia` or `compositeWithTrainedAlgorithmicMedia`, or for a C2PA `c2pa.ai_generated` or `c2pa.actions` entry (the part of a manifest that records how the image was made or edited). A C2PA manifest without one of these entries is recorded in the evidence but does not count.

- **Found:** an image carries one of these markers, so its marking is intact. The evidence names each image and the marker found.
- **Not applicable:** no image carries a marker, or no product image was found. This is not a finding. Missing metadata does not show that an image was made by a camera, because CDNs and image optimisers often strip it.
- **Could not check:** no sampled product page could be read.

What the scanner cannot see:

- Whether a C2PA manifest's signature is valid. The check looks for the marker, not for its cryptographic proof.
- Invisible watermarks embedded in the pixels, which need the watermark vendor's own detector.
- Gallery images other than the main image, images larger than 2 MB and images loaded by JavaScript.

The result is reported, never scored, and it is not legal advice.

## How to fix it

Keep the metadata from the generator to the browser. Start with the original file, then follow it through every step that touches it.

1. Check the original with exiftool or a C2PA reader. If the generator wrote no IPTC value, add `trainedAlgorithmicMedia` or `compositeWithTrainedAlgorithmicMedia` before upload.
2. Turn off "strip metadata" options in optimisation tools for these images.
3. Enable the CDN's option to keep Content Credentials where it has one.
4. Fetch the image URL your product page actually serves and check it again.
5. Where the metadata cannot survive, say in the visible product content that the image was created with AI.

A C2PA manifest describes the exact bytes it was signed for. Resizing or re-encoding changes those bytes, so a manifest survives a transformation only when the tool that transforms the image re-signs it. The IPTC value in XMP is simpler and survives whenever metadata is kept.

### WordPress and WooCommerce

- WordPress creates resized copies of every upload. With the Imagick image editor, it keeps IPTC and XMP profiles in resized images. With the GD editor, it removes them. Ask your host which editor is active.
- Very large uploads are scaled down to a "-scaled" copy that becomes the main image. Check that copy, not only the original.
- Image optimisation plugins often strip metadata by default to save bytes. Look for a "keep metadata" or "preserve EXIF/XMP" setting.

### Shopify

- Shopify serves product images through its own CDN in resized and often converted variants. Fetch the image URL your theme actually uses and check whether the XMP survived.
- If it did not, state on the product page that the image was created with AI, for example in the description or a caption next to the gallery.

### Any other stack

- Configure your image service to keep XMP metadata. Cloudflare Images, for example, has a setting to preserve Content Credentials and appends its own signed record of each transformation.
- Keep metadata handling in your build tools explicit, so a later dependency upgrade does not start stripping it silently.

## Verify it yourself

Download the image your product page serves and inspect it:

```bash
curl -s -o hero.jpg "https://example.com/cdn/products/hero.jpg"
exiftool -XMP-iptcExt:DigitalSourceType -JUMBF:All hero.jpg
c2patool hero.jpg
```

exiftool prints the digital source type and any C2PA (JUMBF) data. c2patool, the Content Authenticity Initiative's command-line tool, prints and validates a manifest. The Content Credentials website at contentcredentials.org/verify does the same in a browser. Compare the served file with your original to see which step removed what.

## Common mistakes

- **Checking only the original.** The file in your media library may be intact while the resized variant on the product page is not.
- **Optimiser defaults.** Metadata stripping is a common default in image optimisation tools.
- **Treating "not applicable" as proof.** It means no marker survived, not that the image is a photograph.
- **Removing markers on purpose.** Stripping a generator's marking defeats the purpose of Article 50 and leaves agents guessing.
- **Marking only in alt text.** Alt text describes the image for people who cannot see it. A note about AI generation belongs in visible content and in the metadata.

## FAQ

### Does this apply to my store?

The marking duty in Article 50(2) binds providers of generative AI systems, such as the makers of image generators, and applies to systems used in the EU. The deep fake disclosure in Article 50(4) binds deployers, which can include a store, when generated content falsely appears authentic. Whether your product images fall under either is a question for a qualified lawyer.

### The check says not applicable. Are my images fine?

The result says only that no image read carried an AI-generation marker. Either the images are not generated, or the marker was stripped on the way. The scan cannot tell which.

### Do I have to label AI-generated product images?

The AI Act's marking duty sits with the tool provider, and its deep fake disclosure depends on the image. Consumer law also prohibits misleading presentations of products. A qualified person can tell you what applies to your images.

### Which tools add these markers?

Many generators attach Content Credentials, among them Adobe Firefly and OpenAI's image models. Editing tools can keep, update or drop them. Check your own tools' documentation and test their output with exiftool or c2patool.
