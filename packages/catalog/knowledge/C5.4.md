## In short

Words such as "eco-friendly", "climate neutral" or "umweltfreundlich" tell a shopper nothing they can check. In the EU, generic environmental claims like these are banned from 27 September 2026 unless the trader can show recognised excellent environmental performance relevant to the claim. This check lists the generic claims it finds on the pages it reads so that a person can review them. It cannot tell whether a claim is backed by evidence.

## What this check looks at

Generic environmental claims are statements of environmental merit that do not say what is better, by how much or according to whom. Three markets regulate them.

- **European Union.** Directive (EU) 2024/825 on empowering consumers for the green transition amends the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (UCPD). Member States must apply it from 27 September 2026. It adds to the UCPD's blacklist of practices that are unfair in all circumstances: generic environmental claims without recognised excellent environmental performance, claims that a product has a neutral, reduced or positive impact on greenhouse gas emissions because of offsetting, sustainability labels that are not based on a certification scheme or set up by public authorities, and claims about a whole product that only concern one aspect of it. A separate proposal, the Green Claims Directive, would have added detailed substantiation rules. Its negotiations stalled in June 2025 after the European Commission announced its intention to withdraw it, and it is not law.
- **United Kingdom.** The Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 (DMCC Act) bans misleading actions and lets the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) fine traders directly since 6 April 2025. The CMA's Green Claims Code explains how that consumer law applies to environmental claims.
- **United States.** The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) Guides for the Use of Environmental Marketing Claims, the Green Guides at 16 CFR Part 260, advise against unqualified general environmental benefit claims because they are difficult to substantiate, and they cover carbon offset claims.

The report lists each provision the check relates to with a link to its official text.

## Why it matters for AI agents

Shoppers ask assistants for "sustainable running shoes" or "a climate-neutral coffee brand". Answer engines build those answers from product pages, and they repeat the claims they read. An unsubstantiated "eco-friendly" on your page becomes a recommendation in someone else's answer, attributed to your store, without the nuance a human editor might add.

A specific claim works better for both sides. "Upper made from 70 % recycled polyester, certified under a named scheme" is an attribute an agent can match against a request, compare across products and cite. "Eco-friendly" is a word it can only repeat. If you hold a certification, schema.org lets you state it on the product with `hasCertification`, which gives agents a structured fact instead of an adjective.

## How the scanner decides

The check runs when the store appears to sell to the EU, the UK or the US. The scanner reads the visible text of the home page and the sampled product pages.

- **Review:** a page uses a generic claim. Examples it recognises are "climate neutral", "carbon neutral", "CO2-neutral", "eco-friendly", "environmentally friendly", "klimaneutral", "umweltfreundlich" and "respectueux de l'environnement", with equivalents in other EU languages. The evidence lists the first claim found on each page, with the text around it and the page it appeared on. Fix it and rescan to see whether the page holds more.
- **Found:** no generic claim was seen on the pages read. In this check, Found means "none seen".

What the scanner cannot see:

- Whether a claim is lawful. A claim backed by recognised excellent environmental performance, such as an official ecolabel, or by a certification scheme can be allowed. The scan matches words, not evidence.
- Claims in images, badges and logos, in content loaded by JavaScript, and on pages it does not read, such as a blog or an "about us" page.

The result is reported, never scored, and it is not legal advice.

## How to fix it

Make an inventory of every environmental claim, then decide claim by claim with the evidence in front of you.

1. Collect claims from product titles and descriptions, category pages, banners, badges, meta descriptions, structured data and product feeds.
2. For each claim, name the evidence: a measurement, a test report or a certificate.
3. Rewrite claims with evidence so they say what is better and by how much, and name the scheme behind it.
4. Remove claims without evidence, and product-level neutrality claims that rest on offsetting.
5. Keep the evidence on file and have the final wording reviewed by a qualified person.

### WordPress and WooCommerce

- Search product content in the admin under Products, and search the database or an export for the claim words to find them in short descriptions, attributes and custom fields.
- Check reusable blocks, patterns, widgets and theme options for site-wide badges such as "Climate neutral shipping".
- Update product feeds and SEO plugin fields too, so the old claim does not live on in meta descriptions.

### Shopify

- Use the admin product search and a CSV export of products to find claims in titles, descriptions and tags.
- Check theme sections and app blocks for badges and trust bars, and metafields that hold product highlights.
- Review the copy that apps add, such as carbon offset apps that place a "carbon neutral" label on the product page or in the cart.

### Any other stack

- Search the product database, templates and translation files for the claim words in every language you sell in.
- Keep claim text in structured product data with a reference to its evidence, so a claim cannot be published without it.
- Re-check product feeds sent to marketplaces and shopping channels, which often keep old descriptions.

## Verify it yourself

Look for generic claims in the HTML of your home page and a product page:

```bash
curl -s https://example.com/ | grep -ioE "climate[- ]neutral|carbon[- ]neutral|co2[- ]neutral|eco[- ]friendly|environmentally friendly|klimaneutral|umweltfreundlich|respectueux de l'environnement"
```

Repeat for a few product pages and in each language version of the site. Then rescan and read the list of claims in the evidence.

## Common mistakes

- **Neutrality through offsets.** "Climate neutral thanks to carbon credits" at product level is one of the claims the EU blacklist names.
- **Self-made labels.** A leaf icon with "Green choice" that no certification scheme or public authority stands behind counts as a sustainability label.
- **Whole product, one aspect.** "Sustainable jacket" when only the lining is recycled.
- **Forgetting the feeds.** Product feeds and marketplace listings keep the old claim long after the website changed.
- **Treating "none seen" as clearance.** The scan reads a sample of pages and only text. Claims elsewhere still count.

## FAQ

### Does this apply to my store?

The EU rules apply to commercial practices aimed at consumers in the EU from 27 September 2026, through each Member State's national law, and they have no size threshold. The UK and US rules concern environmental claims made to consumers in those markets. Whether a particular claim on your store is lawful depends on its wording and evidence, which only a qualified person can assess.

### Can I still say that I support climate projects?

The directive bans claiming that a product has a neutral, reduced or positive climate impact because of offsetting. Its recitals keep room for describing contributions to environmental projects accurately, as long as that is not misleading. Have such wording reviewed.

### Is "sustainable" always banned?

No word is banned on its own. A generic claim is lawful in the EU when the trader can demonstrate recognised excellent environmental performance relevant to the claim, and a specific, substantiated claim is not generic. The check flags the words so a person can make that judgement.

### Why does the scanner flag a claim I can prove?

The scan sees the words, not your evidence. Review the listed claim, and if the evidence is in place, consider making the claim specific on the page so readers and agents can see what it rests on.
