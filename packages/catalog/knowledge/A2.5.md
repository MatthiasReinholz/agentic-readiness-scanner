## In short

When a person asks an assistant about a specific page, the assistant fetches it on the spot with a user-triggered fetcher such as ChatGPT-User or Claude-User. This check reports whether your robots.txt disallows any of seven such fetchers on a product page. Most of them fetch anyway because a person asked, so the rule mostly records an intent you probably do not hold.

## What this check looks at

User-triggered fetchers are different from crawlers. A crawler visits pages on its own schedule to build an index. A user-triggered fetcher requests one page at the moment a person asks for it, for example when someone pastes a product link into a chat and asks "is this in stock in size M?".

The scanner evaluates robots.txt for these product tokens:

| Fetcher              | Operator   |
| -------------------- | ---------- |
| ChatGPT-User         | OpenAI     |
| Claude-User          | Anthropic  |
| Perplexity-User      | Perplexity |
| Google-Agent         | Google     |
| meta-externalfetcher | Meta       |
| Amzn-User            | Amazon     |
| MistralAI-User       | Mistral    |

It uses the path of a sampled product page, or the home page when no product was found, and applies RFC 9309. A fetcher without a group of its own follows the wildcard group `User-agent: *`. So a wildcard `Disallow: /` disallows all seven, even though none is named.

## Why it matters for AI agents

These fetchers are the closest thing today to an agent shopping on a person's behalf. The person has already chosen your store. Blocking the fetch turns a warm lead into an assistant that answers "I could not open that page".

The operators document different behaviour. OpenAI, Perplexity, Meta and Amazon document that their user-triggered fetchers may ignore robots.txt, because a person requested the page. Anthropic documents that Claude-User honours it. Mistral does not say. In practice, a Disallow for these fetchers stops some assistants and is ignored by others. That makes the rule an unreliable control and a clear statement that you would rather people did not ask assistants about your products.

If you want to keep assistants away from part of the site, robots.txt is the wrong tool for most of them. A login wall or server-side rules are what actually stop a fetch.

## How the scanner decides

The check depends on robots.txt being found and parsed (see [robots.txt exists and parses](/knowledge/robots-txt-exists-and-parses)).

- **Pass:** all seven fetchers may fetch the sampled path, or the site has no robots.txt rules.
- **Fail:** any one of the seven is disallowed on that path.

The evidence lists each fetcher with its verdict and the path that was evaluated.

The weight is 1, which means nice to have. The weight is low on purpose: since most of these fetchers do not obey the rule, the fail describes your stated intent more than what assistants actually do. It is not a gate for any level.

## How to fix it

Remove any rule that disallows these fetchers on product, category and policy paths. Keep cart, checkout and account paths disallowed for everyone, which is normal and does not affect this check.

### WordPress and WooCommerce

- Check for a physical `robots.txt` in the web root first, since it overrides the virtual file WordPress generates.
- Edit robots.txt in your SEO plugin's editor. Look for groups that name any of the seven tokens, and for a wildcard `Disallow: /` left over from a staging site.
- Plugins that "block AI bots" often include ChatGPT-User and similar fetchers in their lists. Remove them if you want assistants to be able to open your pages when asked.

### Shopify

- Open `robots.txt.liquid` under Online Store, Themes, Edit code, if the template exists. Shopify's defaults do not disallow these fetchers on product pages.
- Remove groups that name the fetchers with `Disallow: /`, or narrow them to the paths you really want to keep private.

### Any other stack

- Edit robots.txt where it is generated, and check your CDN's managed robots.txt settings as well.
- If you keep a group for the fetchers, give it the same private paths as the wildcard group and nothing more. A named group replaces the wildcard group for that fetcher, so it needs its own `Disallow` lines for cart and checkout.

## Verify it yourself

Look for groups that name the fetchers, and for a blanket rule in the wildcard group:

```bash
curl -s https://example.com/robots.txt | grep -inE -A3 "chatgpt-user|claude-user|perplexity-user|google-agent|meta-externalfetcher|amzn-user|mistralai-user|user-agent: \*"
```

Read the `Disallow` lines under each match and check whether one covers your product paths. Then rescan to confirm.

## Common mistakes

- **Copying a "block all AI" list.** Such lists mix training crawlers, search crawlers and user-triggered fetchers. Each category has a different effect.
- **Leaving a staging rule in place.** `User-agent: *` with `Disallow: /` disallows every fetcher on this list.
- **Expecting robots.txt to enforce a block.** Several operators document that these fetchers may ignore it. Use authentication or server rules if a page must stay private.
- **Forgetting private paths in a named group.** `User-agent: ChatGPT-User` with only `Allow: /` also opens your checkout to it, because the wildcard rules no longer apply.

## FAQ

### Does ChatGPT-User obey robots.txt?

OpenAI documents that ChatGPT-User acts on behalf of a person and that robots.txt rules may not apply to it. A Disallow therefore states a preference rather than enforcing a block.

### Is ChatGPT-User the same as GPTBot?

No. GPTBot is OpenAI's training crawler. ChatGPT-User fetches a page when a person asks ChatGPT about it. OAI-SearchBot builds the index for ChatGPT search. Each has its own robots.txt token.

### Should I block user-triggered fetchers to protect my prices?

robots.txt will not do that reliably, since most of these fetchers may ignore it. Your prices are also visible to any person who opens the page. Blocking mostly stops an assistant from helping someone who already wants to buy from you.

### Why is this check weighted so low?

Because the rule changes little in practice. Most of these fetchers fetch anyway when a person asks. The check flags the rule so you can decide whether it says what you mean.
