## In short

Many consumer and e-commerce laws ask an online seller to say who it is: its legal name, a geographic address where it can be found and an email address that reaches it. This check reads your imprint (the page that says who runs the site, called Impressum in German), your contact page and your home page, and reports whether all three are there. It cannot tell whether the details are true, or whether the law asks your business for more.

## What this check looks at

The three items are the common core of seller-identity rules in many markets. The details differ by market:

- **European Union.** The [E-Commerce Directive](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2000/31/oj), Article 5(1), asks every information society service provider to make its name, the geographic address where it is established and contact details including an email address "easily, directly and permanently accessible". National laws implement it, for example the German Digital Services Act (DDG), §5, which replaced §5 of the Telemedia Act in May 2024, and the Austrian E-Commerce Act (ECG), §5.
- **United Kingdom.** The [Electronic Commerce (EC Directive) Regulations 2002](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2002/2013/regulation/6), regulation 6, keep the same list.
- **Switzerland.** The Unfair Competition Act (UWG), Article 3(1)(s), asks anyone selling online to give clear and complete information about their identity and contact address, including an email address.
- **California.** Civil Code §1789.3 asks providers of electronic commercial services to consumers to give their name, address and telephone number, and the address of the state's Complaint Assistance Unit.
- **Canada.** Ontario's rules for internet agreements under the Consumer Protection Act, 2002 (O. Reg. 17/05, section 32) and Quebec's Consumer Protection Act, section 54.4, ask the merchant to disclose its name, address, telephone number and, where available, email address before a distance contract.
- **Brazil.** [Decreto 7.962/2013](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2011-2014/2013/decreto/d7962.htm), Article 2, asks for the business name and CNPJ or CPF number, a physical and an electronic address, in a prominent, easy-to-see place.
- **Chile and Colombia.** Chile's Consumer Protection Act (Ley 19.496) and its e-commerce regulation, and Colombia's Consumer Statute (Ley 1480 de 2011), Article 50, ask for the seller's name, tax number, address and contact details.

Several of these laws ask for more than the three items, such as a register number, a VAT ID or a telephone number. The [registration and tax numbers check](/knowledge-base/business-registration-and-tax-numbers) covers the numbers. The page lists every law this check is tagged with.

## Why it matters for AI agents

Shopping agents and answer engines decide which stores to trust, recommend and cite. "Who is behind this shop?" is one of the first questions they try to answer, for the shopper and for themselves. A legal name and a geographic address let an agent match the store to a real business in company registers, reviews and other sources. An email address gives it, and the shopper, a way to reach the seller after the sale.

An agent can only use what it can read. A clear identity block in the server-rendered HTML can be quoted directly. Organization markup with `legalName`, `address` as a `PostalAddress` and `email` states the same facts without depending on layout or language, which the [merchant identity check](/knowledge-base/merchant-identity) covers from the readiness side. A store that hides its identity behind a contact form looks, to a machine, much like one that has none.

## How the scanner decides

The scanner reads the imprint or legal-notice page and the contact page it found in the home page links, and always the home page as well. It also reads the Organization JSON-LD (structured data in the page that machines read) on those pages. It looks for three things:

1. **A legal name.** A company form in the text, such as GmbH, Ltd, LLC, SAS, S.A. or Ltda., or the `legalName` or `name` of an Organization node.
2. **A postal address.** A street name with a house number or a postcode next to a place name in the text, or a `PostalAddress` with a `streetAddress` in the markup.
3. **An email address.** An address in the text, a `mailto:` link, or an `email` on the Organization or its `contactPoint`.

The verdicts:

- **Found:** all three are present.
- **Review:** one or two are present.
- **Not found in the pages read:** none is present.

A contact form alone does not count as an email address. The scanner reads the HTML the server sends and does not run JavaScript, so details that only appear after scripts run, inside an image or behind a cookie banner are not seen. It cannot check whether the details are true, current or complete, or whether your business needs to publish more. This check is reported, never scored.

## How to fix it

Publish one page, usually called Imprint, Legal notice, Impressum or Mentions légales, with your full legal name including the company form, the geographic address where the business is established and an email address that someone reads. Link it from the footer of every page. Add the same details to your Organization markup. Have the wording reviewed by a lawyer who knows the markets you sell to, because many laws ask for more than these three items.

### WordPress and WooCommerce

- Create a page under Pages, Add New, named "Legal notice" or "Imprint", and write the details as plain text, not as an image.
- Link it from the footer menu under Appearance, Menus, or in the footer template part in the Site Editor for block themes.
- Fill in the organisation settings of your SEO plugin with the legal name, address and email, so the Organization JSON-LD carries them. The store address under WooCommerce, Settings, General does not reach the markup on its own.

### Shopify

- Fill in Settings, Policies, Contact information. Shopify publishes it at `/policies/contact-information`. Enter the legal name, address and email address there as text.
- Link that page, or a page you create under Online Store, Pages, from the footer menu under Online Store, Navigation.
- Check that the store's legal name and address under Settings, Store details match what the page says.

### Any other stack

- Serve the identity page at a stable URL such as `/imprint` or `/legal-notice` and link it from a `footer` element in the server-rendered HTML.
- Write the email address as text or as a `mailto:` link. Spam protection that builds the address with JavaScript hides it from the scanner and from agents.
- Output an Organization node with `legalName`, `address` as a `PostalAddress` and `email`, and keep it identical to the page.

## Verify it yourself

Fetch your identity page as the server sends it and look for an email address and a postcode:

```bash
curl -s https://example.com/imprint | sed -e 's/<[^>]*>/ /g' | grep -oiE '[a-z0-9._%+-]+@[a-z0-9.-]+\.[a-z]{2,}|[0-9]{4,5} [A-Z][a-z]+' | sort -u
```

Then paste the page URL into the Schema Markup Validator at validator.schema.org and check that the Organization has a `legalName`, an `address` and an `email`. Rescan with this scanner after the change.

## Common mistakes

- **A contact form instead of an email address.** Several laws ask for an email address, and the scanner does not treat a form as one.
- **A PO box as the only address.** The laws above ask for the geographic address where the business is established.
- **The trading name without the legal name.** "Example Ceramics" alone does not say which company sells. Add the company form, such as "Example Ceramics GmbH".
- **The imprint as an image or PDF.** Neither the scanner nor most agents read text inside images.
- **Different details in different places.** A legal name in the imprint that differs from the markup and the terms makes the business harder to match.

## FAQ

### Does this apply to my store?

It depends on where you are established and where you sell. The EU and UK rules apply to providers of online services established there, whether they sell to consumers or businesses and regardless of size. The Swiss, Californian, Canadian and Latin American rules above concern selling to consumers in those markets. The scanner runs this check for every store, because a seller identity block is expected in almost every market, and it cannot tell which rules apply to your business.

### Is an About page enough?

It can be, if it states the legal name, a geographic address and an email address in text. The scanner does not read an About page. It reads the imprint, legal-notice and contact pages linked from the home page, so link the details with a label such as "Legal notice" or "Imprint".

### Can I use a PO box or a virtual office?

A PO box is not a geographic address. Whether a virtual office or registered agent address satisfies a given law is a question for a lawyer. The scanner only checks that something shaped like a street address or a postcode is present.

### Do I have to show my home address as a sole trader?

Several laws ask for the address where the business is established, which for some sole traders is their home. Whether an alternative is acceptable depends on the market. The scanner accepts any street address or postcode it recognises.

### What is the difference between this check and the merchant identity check?

The [merchant identity check](/knowledge-base/merchant-identity) is part of the readiness score and asks whether agents can read who the merchant is. This check is a legal signal: it asks whether the three items many laws name are all present, and it is never scored.
